Abstract This survey examines the recent developments in adaptive multiple-choice question (MCQ)
assessment systems where the learner performance data is used to offer customized assessment. The re-
search concentrates on the compatibility of adaptive assessments with Bloom taxonomy in matching
questions with the answer to the questions and dynamically responding to the answers by the students.
Automatic multiple-choice question generation, cognitive-level classification, bias detection and adap-
tive difficulty adjustment are thoroughly reviewed in the literature. The results reveal that adaptive
methods have the potential to enhance formative assessment tremendously through heightening the rele-
vance of questions, facilitating fair treatment, and timely feedback. Nevertheless, the continuous issues
related to the quality of questions, proper cognitive alignment, minimization of bias, and the credibility
of instant feedback systems are also mentioned in the literature. Overall, this review offers substantial
points of departure to the creation of effective and fair adaptive assessment systems by summarizing
existing approaches and outlining the gaps in the research. Besides integrating the available literature
in the adaptive assessment technologies, the research will also introduce a generalized and modular
workflow system, which incorporates the content preprocessing, the alignment to the Bloom taxonomy,
the adaptive sequencing, mitigation of bias, or feedback generation into a single architectural design.
The framework is a loose outline that can be used by researchers to fit different fields of education and
contexts of implementation.
Keywords: AI-Powered Adaptive Assessment, Bloom’s Taxonomy, Dynamic Question Gen- eration,
Cognitive Level Classification, Personalization, Intelligent Assessment Systems.

Introduction
AI testing has significantly revolutionized the view and activity of learning and assessment [42]. Modern
educational practices are progressively incorporating AI-based algorithms to automatically create and
remodel various multiple-choice questions (MCQs) according to cognitively accepted models like the
Bloom Taxonomy [3]. This has substituted the stagnant test banks and manual grading by dynamic
systems that can produce individualized tests as well as provide intelligent feedback that is dynamic and
changes in real time based on the performance of a learner. With the evaluation of the goals oriented to
the progressive levels of cognition, basic recall to higher-level reasoning, AI systems tend to encourage
the student to gain new ideas instead of memorizing [53]. In addition, the development of generative
models and natural language processing has made it possible to transform assessments into interactive,
student-focused exercises that no longer distinguish between instruction and assessment [16].
But, in conjunction with these developments, there is an increasing demand to use AI-generated
questions to design questions, and this is a serious problem that quickly arises: the design of automat-
ically generated MCQs is becoming obscurant. In contrast to the conventional test, which is carefully
reviewed by the pedagogue, AI-generated questions can include unintentionally ambiguous linguistic
statements, multiple possible answers, or redundant ones. These problems may mislead learners, im-
precisely assess knowledge and violate the reliability of automated grading systems. This is even more
pronounced in adaptive testing settings, where a wrong answer can be an indication of poor interpretation
and not poor knowledge, which causes the system to scale the difficulty levels or learning trajectories,
in the wrong way. In the long term, such errors can defraud the profile of learners, introduce cumulative
error in learning and decline confidence in AI-based evaluation systems.
Ambiguity is also directly related to bias detection and mitigation. Assessment systems based on AI
might be prone to implicit biases due to differences in language, imbalances in training data used, or
construct-irrelevant inquiries [67]. The unclear wording may prejudice the learners of different linguistic
or cognitive experiences with disproportionate representation, which is why one may question the notion
of fairness, inclusivity, and transparency. As a result, the current literature on the topic highlights the
fact that ambiguity and bias should be identified, measured, and reduced as a system of study in order to
make adaptive MCQ systems equitable, interpretable, and pedagogically valid [68].
In order to mitigate these issues, it is important to develop a solid student engagement interface that
will allow real time engagement between students and assessment system. A layer of this kind may
dynamically track the performance trends, modify the level of difficulty and the level of clarity of the
questions, and give practical feedback to aid in the learning process [18]. Teachers have the advantage of
getting specific analytics that inform their teaching techniques and interventions and students automat-
ically get feedback that lets them detect misunderstandings and deepen their conceptual understanding.
Interactive AI-based assessment systems can increase the motivation of learners, their engagement, and
long-term educational outcomes to a large extent when they are developed in a way that boosts their
clarity and interpretability [25] [35].
In this regard, the current work presents a new quiz-generation workflow architecture that is based
on AI and represents the main contribution of this study. Instead of comparing the model performance or
single tool performances, we introduce a more organized seven-layer workflow framework that system-
2
atically arranges the entire MCQ generation pipeline, such as the data acquisition and model selection
processes and such like the ambiguity detection, bias reduction, evaluation, adaptive delivery, and feed-
back optimization. On a case-by-case basis, we thoroughly examine the possible tools, models, as well
as large language model (LLM) settings, summarizing their functionality, strengths, weaknesses, and
trade-offs. The comparative information is given to help researchers to choose those components which
suit their particular pedagogical and technical needs the most. Using the ability to combine all these
opportunities into a single workflow, the current paper provides a practical and research-focused manual
that can empower scholars and system designers to deploy powerful, interpretable, and superior AI-
enabled MCQ generation systems. The proposed workflow is therefore considered to be a systematic
decision support model that allows the researchers to be in a position of producing the best results with
minimal ambiguity and bias in adaptive assessment settings.

Survey Methodology
Figure 1 illustrates the general workflow of the adaptive AI-driven system for generating and assessing
MCQs. The system follows a structured end-to-end architecture, beginning with learning material input
and concluding with feedback and recommendation mechanisms. The architecture is organized into
three major stages: (1) Input and Processing, (2) Question Generation and Validation, and (3) Adaptive
Delivery and User Interaction.
The workflow begins with the ingestion of learning materials, which undergo preprocessing before
being transformed into multiple-choice questions using large language models. The generated questions
are subsequently classified according to cognitive levels and evaluated for bias to ensure pedagogical
alignment and fairness. Validated questions are then delivered through an adaptive engine that dynami-
cally adjusts difficulty levels and topic coverage based on learner performance. The student interaction
layer records responses, which drive continuous feedback and recommendation mechanisms.
To synthesize findings from published literature on AI-based assessment, adaptive MCQ generation,
Bloom’s Taxonomy classification, bias detection, intelligent tutoring systems, learning analytics, and
computerized adaptive testing, this survey adopts a structured narrative review methodology aligned
with the proposed workflow. Literature was retrieved using targeted keyword combinations from IEEE
Xplore, ACM Digital Library, SpringerLink, ScienceDirect, Scopus, and Google Scholar. Backward
and forward citation analysis was conducted to identify influential foundational studies and emerging
research trends.
The review first examines research related to learning content ingestion and preprocessing tech-
niques, including data cleaning, normalization, feature extraction, and domain-sensitive representation,
as these directly influence downstream semantic quality. It then evaluates studies on MCQ generation us-
ing large language models, comparing proprietary and open-source generators in terms of linguistic co-
herence, reasoning capability, scalability, and deployment constraints. Research on Bloom’s Taxonomy
classification is analyzed, covering classical machine learning, deep learning, and transformer-based
cognitive-level tagging approaches.
Bias detection literature is also synthesized, including positional bias, anchoring bias, symbol-
binding inconsistencies, distractor similarity bias, and mitigation strategies such as permutation diag-
3
nostics and debiasing frameworks. The survey further investigates adaptive question engines grounded
in computerized adaptive testing models and deep knowledge tracing architectures, focusing on real-
time difficulty adjustment and learner state estimation. Finally, student interaction systems, response
analytics, and AI-driven feedback mechanisms are examined to evaluate personalization, interpretabil-
ity, engagement, and pedagogical effectiveness across the assessment lifecycle.
These components collectively form a linear yet interdependent system architecture in which each
stage influences the performance of subsequent phases. The quality of preprocessing and content rep-
resentation directly impacts semantic coherence and conceptual validity of generated MCQs. Cognitive
classification ensures alignment with instructional objectives, while bias detection safeguards fairness
and assessment integrity before deployment. The adaptive engine relies on validated and cognitively
tagged items for informed sequencing decisions, and learner responses captured through the interaction
layer provide performance signals that refine ongoing adaptation. Response analytics transform raw in-
teraction data into interpretable learner metrics, which are then used to generate targeted feedback and
recommendations. Together, these interconnected processes establish a closed-loop adaptive evaluation
framework in which content quality, pedagogical alignment, fairness validation, adaptive sequencing,
learner interaction, and personalized feedback operate as mutually reinforcing components of an inte-
grated intelligent assessment system.

Literature Review
3.1
Learning Content Input
Mayer and Fiorella (2021) developed the cognitive theory of multimedia learning, showing that effective
learning is achieved when students perform five key cognitive processes: selecting words and images
that are relevant, organizing them into coherent models, and relating these models to prior knowledge.
Their work indicates that presentations that integrate words and images in multimedia presentations
produce better transfer test performance than those that are one modality in nature, with effect sizes
ranging from 0.3 to 0.6 across a wide range of subject areas. The theoretical underpinning rests upon
three basic assumptions: dual-channel processing of verbal and visual information, cognitive capacity
as being limited, and active construction of mental representations as opposed to passive reception of
information.
Zou, Lambert, and Xie (2025) performed a thorough examination of learning in the 21st century
that proved how technologies like Smart Sparrow and ALEKS use data analytics to design adaptive,
learner-tailored paths that shift dynamically to meet the unique needs of individual students. Their work
indicates that students using adaptive learning platforms in gateway math courses posted considerably
higher pass rates and exhibited higher overall student engagement than similar students in traditional
learning settings. The research highlights the fact that co-constructed peer-to-peer learning, using col-
laborative digital tools such as Google Classroom, Microsoft Teams, and Padlet, allows for knowledge
building with students generating higher quality assignments and higher levels of engagement with the
course content.
Diwan, Srinivasa, and Bhat (2023) present an AI-driven solution to generate "narrative fragments"–
supportive learning materials strategically placed along learning paths to maintain motivation and so-
lidify key concepts. The system uses natural language processing and machine learning to monitor
learner success, identify knowledge gaps, and generate context-aware micro-content that supports pri-
mary learning achievements.Empirical research proved that students exposed to the AI-augmented learn-
ing system recorded 23% superior performance on assessment tasks and 31% increased engagement
rates when compared to control groups, proving that AI-powered content creation is a feasible solution
to the scalability issues of personalized education.
Bankar et al. (2023) offer a holistic review of e-content development in medical education, un-
derlining the central role digital learning materials play in healthcare education environments. The
study proves that well-crafted e-content really does make a difference in student participation and learn-
ing outcomes, as medical students utilizing structured e-content modules score 18% higher on clinical
exams than students taught by traditional lecture-based method. Essential features of effective medi-
cal e-content include interactive multimedia components mimicking clinical presentations, case-based
learning activities building diagnostic reasoning ability, virtual patient simulations offering a safe prac-
tice space, and adaptive assessment tools that detect knowledge deficits to be remediated.
Maity and Deroy (2024) investigated the integration of Generative AI, particularly large language
models like GPT-4, into Intelligent Tutoring Systems to enhance personalized education through dy-
namic content generation, real-time feedback, and adaptive learning pathways. Their research explores
6
key applications including automated question generation, customized feedback mechanisms, and inter-
active dialogue systems that respond to individual learner needs. The study demonstrates that generative
AI enables highly personalized and adaptive learning environments, with automated systems capable of
creating diverse educational resources tailored to individual learning paces and styles, though challenges
remain in ensuring pedagogical accuracy and mitigating inherent biases in AI models.
3.2
Content Preprocessing
Rahhali, Oughdir, and El Khattabi (2024) conduct a thorough review of preprocessing methods for
education big data, highlighting that preprocessing is an essential process that heavily influences the
accuracy and reliability of learning analytics outcomes. The research divides preprocessing methods
into three broad categories: data cleaning (giving irrelevant data a miss, correcting mistakes, dealing
with missing values), data transformation (transforming data into appropriate forms), and data reduc-
tion (dimension reduction without sacrificing essential information). Research demonstrates that proper
preprocessing can improve educational analytics accuracy by 20-30% compared to raw data analysis,
underscoring the fundamental significance of this often-overlooked stage in the analytics pipeline.
Amrieh, Hamtini, and Aljarah (2016) introduced a comprehensive student performance prediction
model incorporating behavioral features captured through Experience API (xAPI) from the Kalboard
360 e-learning system. Their research categorized features into demographic, academic background, and
behavioral categories, with behavioral features representing learner experiences during the educational
process. When three classification methods (Artificial Neural Networks, Naïve Bayes, and Decision
Trees) were applied, the inclusion of properly preprocessed behavioral features achieved remarkable
improvements: ANN showed 25-29% accuracy improvement, Naïve Bayes achieved 22% improvement,
and Decision Tree demonstrated 6-7% improvement, revealing that feature choice and preprocessing
quality can have effects comparable to or exceeding algorithm selection.
Križani´c (2020) demonstrated the critical role of content preprocessing through a comprehensive
case study analyzing event logs from an e-learning course. Event logs were cleansed, transformed,
and grouped to model student behavior effectively, with preprocessing steps including data cleaning,
transformation, and partitioning—all crucial for revealing meaningful patterns in educational data. The
study applied k-means cluster analysis to classify students according to learning patterns, then decision
tree analysis for predictive outcomes. Well-preprocessed data enabled precise prediction of student
performance and identification of risk groups, facilitating timely adaptive interventions.
Romero and Ventura (2020) conducted a comprehensive survey on educational data mining, high-
lighting the critical importance of data preprocessing as a foundational step for effective knowledge
discovery in educational datasets. Their research emphasizes that educational data presents unique pre-
processing challenges including high dimensionality, class imbalance, temporal dependencies, and the
need for domain-specific feature engineering. The study demonstrates that successful educational data
mining applications systematically address data quality issues through careful preprocessing, with tech-
niques including outlier detection, feature selection, dimensionality reduction, and temporal aggregation
proving essential for extracting actionable insights from learning management system logs and student
interaction data.
Shahiri, Husain, and Rashid (2015) investigated data preprocessing techniques for predicting stu-
7
dent performance using machine learning algorithms, emphasizing the impact of feature selection and
engineering on prediction accuracy. Their research applied various preprocessing methods including
handling missing values, feature normalization, and feature selection using correlation-based techniques
to educational datasets containing student demographic, academic, and behavioral information. The
study demonstrated that careful preprocessing and feature engineering improved prediction accuracy by
15-20%, with feature selection algorithms successfully identifying the most predictive attributes while
reducing computational complexity and avoiding overfitting in student performance models.
3.3
MCQ Generator using LLMs
Al-gburi (2025) introduces APUOPE-RE, an adaptive quiz generator model designed especially for re-
quirements engineering learning resources that dynamically modifies question difficulty based on each
learner’s performance. Using a four-stage architecture that includes retrieval and mapping of lecture
content into databases, prompt engineering and item generation by LLaMA 2-13B with specified output
and checking rules, structural checking of generated items, and automated answer checking with in-
stantaneous feedback, the system generates single-choice and true/false questions and SCQs. By giving
harder questions for correct responses and easier ones for incorrect ones, a dynamic difficulty mod-
ule assesses user response patterns and modifies learning progress. Additionally, the system displays
user performance graphically in the form of interactive graphs that monitor improvement pathways over
time, allowing for ongoing incentive and self-evaluation. The architecture achieves a balance between
question type, complexity, and context of relevance through iterative tuning, and additional control
mechanisms are added to stabilise LLM non-determinism and improve output consistency. However,
the framework’s application to other fields is still lacking, and its scope is currently limited to require-
ments engineering. Concerns about the consistency of its measuring standards also include the use of
subjective user-based performance measures and the absence of strict validation methods for content
produced using LLM. Despite these drawbacks, APUOPE-RE is an organised combination of adaptive
learning principles and test design based on LLM that reflects the potential of AI systems in assessing
personalised education.
Biancini et al. (2024) compared three large language models: Llama 2, Mistral, and GPT-3.5; to
generate multiple-choice questions (MCQs) in schools with emphasis on knowledge injection to re-
duce hallucinations. In order to allow teachers to have control over content correctness and conform
to source material, the information was included within the prompts themselves instead of relying on
internal model knowledge. High school-level items must be developed by the models employing the
same source quotation, question-and-option format, and correct option with explanation. Five important
characteristics along which the assessment was performed were learning usefulness, response coherence
and accuracy, quality of distractors, alignment with source, and design clarity. Substantial differences
in performance were observed with a non-parametric Friedman test, where GPT-3.5 performed better
than Llama 2 and Mistral in all but two areas. While anchoring generation to provided content boosted
factual reliability and transparency, it also placed pragmatic constraints. The process restricted au-
tomation and scalability with enormous data since it involved teacher engagement and human-designed
reminders. Generalisability of the findings was hindered by the study’s exceptional topic and limited
sample size, and the teachers’ understanding of acceptability was undermined by not receiving demo-
8
graphic data. Additionally, application of textual quotes at times undermined contextual knowledge,
and unavailability of human-derived baselines made it hard to establish the real instructional value of
generated multiple-choice questions.
Sahu et al. (2023) explain transformer-based models, i.e., GPT and BERT, for automatically gener-
ating multiple-choice questions (MCQs) with emphasis on system design and evaluation strategy. Their
method uses automated grammar checkers, reading comprehension systems, and reasoning-based diffi-
culty predictions to evaluate generated questions on four main dimensions: grammaticality, answerabil-
ity, diversity, and complexity. BERT generates semantically identical distractors that are highly relevant
to the right answer by using masked language modelling to forecast omitted tokens in text. On the other
hand, GPT-3, which was trained on text-question pairings, generates complete question-answer pairs in a
single generative phase by performing end-to-end MCQ production. In order to provide coherent, varied,
and contextually authentic assessments, the research also explores hybrid techniques that combine the
generative expressiveness of GPT with the bidirectional contextual sense of BERT. Combining comple-
mentary transformer strengths raises a number of technical and practical challenges while maintaining
grammatical accuracy and contextual validity. Scalability and sustainability may be hampered by the
massive processing resources required by both architectures. While GPT’s open-ended production runs
the danger of producing realistic but inaccurate results, BERT may occasionally misestimate ambiguous
or implicit conditions. The study also highlights the ongoing challenge of producing distractors that are
really plausible without confusing learners, as well as the shortcomings of conventional n-gram metrics
in assessing MCQ task semantic richness.
Rodriguez-Torrealba et al. (2022) introduce an end-to-end framework for generating multiple-choice
question (MCQ) that uses pre-trained T5 models to convert English Wikipedia articles to structured ex-
ams. The system is based on three fundamental modules: Question Generation (QG), Question Answer-
ing (QA), and Distractor Generation (DG), deployed via a five-step pipeline. Wikipedia content is first
parsed into meaningful paragraphs, and then question–answer pairs are generated through a fine-tuned
T5-based QAPModel. Distractors are then generated with the help of a special DGModel, their semantic
closeness to correct answers evaluated based on cosine similarity via GloVe embeddings. The output
questions and answers are then aggregated into a uniform JSON structure, guaranteeing structural con-
sistency. The DG component has a Text-to-Text formulation in which the model generates distractors
conditioned on question, answer, and context inputs. Training employed optimized hyperparameters and
beam search in addition to top-k and top-p sampling techniques to improve output diversity and creativ-
ity. The single Text-to-Text paradigm allows for frictionless adaptation between sub-tasks and supports
reproducibility through available models and code. Yet the results of evaluation show the shortcom-
ings of word-overlap metrics like BLEU, which do not effectively reflect semantic depth but return low
scores. While distractor similarity scores reflect significant relatedness, over-similarity among alterna-
tives threatens vagueness. Furthermore, dependence on extractive generation limits the ability of the
system to deal with reasoning-dense or multi-hop questions and thus its overall cognitive scope and
generalization capacity.
Hang et al. (2024) suggest MCQGen, a complete system integrating GPT-4 with Retrieval-Augmented
Generation (RAG) and advanced prompt engineering techniques for MCQ generation. The system is
supported by a two-pronged prompt engineering approach using Chain-of-Thought (CoT) and self-refine
9
strategies to prompt the LLM to go through sequential refinement processes. The methodology begins
by requesting instructors to supply domain-specific facts that populate an external knowledge base with
605 MCQs. RAG dynamically provided access to this database at question time for question generation,
extracting relevant information for completing the generation context. CoT methodology encourages se-
quential logical reasoning by utilizing the generation of candidate distractors as a meta-goal, evaluating
the plausibility of these, establishing final choice based on common misconceptions. The self-improve
approach establishes a loop where GPT-4 judges and enhances its initial response to increase difficulty
without altering correctness. It was experimented on three question categories: math, concept, and code-
based according to criteria of grammatical ease, answerability, diversity, complexity, and relevance. The
major advantage of this system is its holistic blend of several approaches, which guarantees questions are
contextually appropriate, challenging, and rooted in student misconceptions, thereby enabling personal-
ized learning at scale. The system demonstrated high performance in grammatical fluidity and relevance
on all question types. But there were significant failings in math question creation, where GPT-4’s poor
reasoning capacity in numbers resulted in instances where correct answers were not among the choices.
The model was equally poor in question diversity and depth, attributed to constraints of the prompt
engineering process and scope of external knowledge base. In addition, sole focus on GPT-4 without
comparative analysis of other models and restriction to English-language computer science writings
limits the generalizability of findings.
3.3.1
Bloom’s Taxonomy Classifier
Abduljabbar and Omar (2015) proposed a hybrid machine learning model that utilizes a voting algorithm
to classify exam questions based on Bloom’s cognitive taxonomy by integrating Support Vector Ma-
chine (SVM), Naïve Bayes (NB) , and k-Nearest Neighbor classifiers (KNN). Their approach improves
classifier performance by employing feature selection approaches including Mutual Information, Chi-
square, and Odd Ratio to address the problem of classifying brief programming-related queries. They
showed that the voting ensemble outperforms individual classifiers by utilizing their distinct strengths,
with SVM surpassing k-NN in the Comprehension and Analysis categories and k-NN excelling in the
Knowledge, Application, Synthesis, and Evaluation categories, particularly when combined with Mu-
tual Information-based feature selection. By demonstrating that a mixed classifier architecture offers
more accuracy and robustness, the study improves on earlier rule based and single model classification
techniques and better supports exam quality control and assessment design.
Osadi et al. (2017) created an ensemble classifier to categorize exam questions into Bloom’s Taxon-
omy cognitive tiers by combining rule based, Support Vector Machine (SVM), Naïve Bayes (NB), and
k-Nearest Neighbour (k-NN) classifiers with majority voting and enhancing them using WordNet se-
mantic similarity. By using WordNet to capture semantic relationships in questions, the study addressed
the issues of overlapping keywords and a lack of semantic understanding in earlier works. They evalu-
ated the model on a dataset of first year programming exam questions from the University of Colombo.
According to their findings, the ensemble method performed noticeably better than individual classifiers
at every cognitive level in terms of accuracy, precision, recall, and F-measure. Among classifiers, the
ensemble achieved the highest overall accuracy, indicating that merging varied classifiers compensates
for individual deficiencies and boosts resilience. According to the study’s findings, majority voting ef-
10
ficiently capitalizes on classifier strengths, and semantic similarity integration is essential for lowering
classification ambiguity. They underlined that this approach supports balanced exam design by offering
an accurate automated classification.
Chanaa et al. (2024) provide a thorough analysis of the literature and an empirical investigation
into the automated assessment of cognitive level in online tests utilizing machine learning and Bloom’s
taxonomy. The authors draw attention to the drawbacks of manual coding for cognitive level analysis,
pointing out that it is time consuming and not scalable. To address this, they compare several supervised
machine learning models—including neural networks (NN), support vector machines (SVM), gradient
boosting trees (GBT), random forests (RF), logistic regression (LR), and naive Bayes (NB) using a
large, expert-annotated dataset of 15,182 learner assessment records. Their method predicts learners’
cognitive levels (low, medium, and high) in real time by combining cognitive and affective factors in
a novel way. These features include time spent, hint usage, attempt count, problem solving sequence,
and confusion score. Neural networks outperformed other techniques and showed strong classification
ability, achieving the greatest accuracy (82.2%) of all tested models. According to the study’s findings,
ML-based cognitive level assessment is useful and efficient for adaptive online learning, allowing for
more individualized pedagogical interventions and better learning results.
Naik et al. (2025) conducted a systematic comparison of machine learning models to automate the
classification of exam questions according to Bloom’s Taxonomy. The authors used efficient natural
language processing techniques, such as RAKE for keyword extraction, SpaCy for tokenization and
lemmatization, Regex for text cleaning, and TF-IDF for feature vectorization, to preprocess a dataset
of 3,849 questions labeled across all six cognitive levels. A wide range of machine learning tech-
niques were then assessed, including Random Forest, K-Nearest Neighbors, Logistic Regression, Linear
Discriminant Analysis, Support Vector Machine, Multinomial Naive Bayes, and Decision Tree. The
Random Forest classifier was ultimately found to be the most effective model, with 90% accuracy and
balanced precision, recall, and F1 scores across all Bloom’s levels. The authors acknowledge Random
Forest’s improved performance to its resilience to overfitting and its capacity to manage intricate, high-
dimensional feature spaces.
Shaikh et al. (2021) developed and assessed a deep learning model based on Long Short-Term
Memory (LSTM) networks. Two manually tagged datasets were created by the authors, who then used
pre-trained Wiki Word Vectors to extract semantic features after performing substantial text preparation,
which included lowercasing, stopword removal, punctuation cleaning, tokenization, and lemmatization.
The LSTM model was selected because it can handle the problem of overlapping action verbs that
confuse simpler models, as well as long-term dependencies and contextual linkages in sequential text.
Through methodical tests, the LSTM model was contrasted with keyword based strategies and conven-
tional machine learning classifiers like Random Forests, Support Vector Machines, Multinomial Naive
Bayes, Logistic Regression, and Artificial Neural Networks. Outperforming all baselines and prior
research, the LSTM model obtained the highest classification accuracy, 87% for CLOs and 74% for
assessment questions. The authors credit this achievement to the sequential modeling capabilities of the
LSTM and the application of strong word embeddings, which work together to provide more accurate
and contextually aware categorization.
Sucipto et al. (2024) use data mining and knowledge discovery in databases (KDD) techniques to
11
provide a thorough assessment of question categorization techniques based on Bloom’s taxonomy. In
order to categorize educational problems into Bloom’s cognitive levels, the authors examine 22 publi-
cations that were published between 2016 and 2023 and encompass hybrid models, deep learning, and
standard machine learning. Naive Bayes (NB), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Support Vector Machines
(SVM), Decision Trees (DT), Logistic Regression (LR), and Random Forests (RF) are examples of
widely used classical models. Of these, KNN and SVM often produce the best results. Because deep
learning models, such as CNN, LSTM, Bi-LSTM, and BERT, can capture contextual and semantic in-
formation, they are becoming more and more popular. In fact, multiple studies have shown that CNN
and LSTM models can achieve accuracy rates of over 85%. To further improve classification perfor-
mance, hybrid strategies are also investigated, such as combining SVM with CNN or RNN. According
to the review, multi-class classification which includes all of Bloom’s levels is difficult because of data
imbalance and overlapping class features, even if single-class classification jobs often have higher ac-
curacy. No single methodology consistently performs better than others across all model types; instead,
variations in accuracy are frequently ascribed to preprocessing quality, feature extraction methods such
as TF-IDF and word embeddings and dataset characteristics.
Manjushree D. Laddha et al. (2021) suggested a deep learning-based method that focused on both
cognitive processes and knowledge dimensions in order to categorize summative assessment questions
using the Revised Bloom’s Taxonomy (RBT). They employed two models Convolutional Neural Net-
works (CNN) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM). While LSTMs, which capture sequential con-
text, sought to comprehend broader meanings, CNNs, which are renowned for catching local features
regardless of position, successfully identified crucial terms in queries. In addition to performing better
in knowledge dimension categorization during testing, the CNN model surpassed LSTM in cognitive
process classification tests, with an accuracy of 80% versus 71% for LSTM. LSTM had poorer test-
ing accuracy due to overfitting, despite having superior training accuracy for knowledge dimensions.
According to the study’s findings, CNN performs better on real-world classification tasks. With the
potential to be expanded to other courses and incorporate metacognitive elements, this study helps in-
structors comprehend the learning outcomes of their students and enables the creation of examinations
that are in line with cognitive and knowledge levels.
3.3.2
Bias Detection
Xue et al. (2024) examine re-emergence of selection bias with fine-tuned large language models su-
pervised on multiple-choice question tasks and attribute it to a great extent to poor Multiple Choice
Symbol Binding (MCSB) capability. Employing LLaMA2-7B and LLaMA2-13B, the research presents
two output configurations: symbols alone and symbols and contents, using the Proportion of Plurality
Agreement (PPA) as its measure. For reducing bias, three successive approaches are presented: Symbol-
Content Binding (SCB), processing both option symbols and contents; Reweighted Symbol-Content
Binding (RSCB), using Focal loss to reweight the salient label words; and Point-wise Intelligent Feed-
back (PIF), using reinforcement learning techniques through the generation of negative samples that
confound the wrong contents with symbols. They are shown to be flexible for various architectures and
datasets without structural changes. But experiments are limited to small models because of computa-
tional resource constraints, and the work makes use of only one negative sample per instance, without
12
considering the effect of changing ratios of samples. SCB also sometimes increases bias in bigger mod-
els such as LLaMA2-13B, and PIF’s employment of pre-determined RSCB parameters adds further
implementation restrictions. Emphasis on requirements engineering further limits the generalizability
of findings to this environment.
Li and Gao (2025) describe a mechanistic interpretability analysis of the phenomenon of anchored
bias in the GPT-2 family of models, in which the models systematically prefer the first option (Ä¨) in
multiple-choice situations regardless of the correctness of the choice. Using the logit lens method,
the authors trace bias propagations across Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) layers and attention heads
in benchmark sets such as Indirect Object Identification (IOI), Logical Deduction, Greater-than, ARC-
Challenge, and CommonsenseQA. Their proposed mitigation strategy combines explicit MLP value
vector adaptation based on knowledge neuron ideas with an attention recalibration mechanism reallo-
cating attention across anchored and correct answer positions. Their approach provides a neuron-level,
systematic description of bias processes and demonstrates how small, targeted modifications can influ-
ence model behavior without retraining or architectural modification. The testing, however, is restricted
to GPT-2 models ranging from 124M to 1.5B parameters, not including larger models such as GPT-3
and LLaMA. Furthermore, while effective in bias localization, interventions degrade non-MCQ perfor-
mance and give a partial picture by largely focusing on failure cases. Few-shot environments are also
inconstant in suppressing anchored bias across all the datasets, particularly for GPT2-XL.
Zheng et al. (2023) investigate the selection bias in multiple-choice answer settings of large lan-
guage models (LLMs) in that some option positions are systematically favored and model performance
is answer order-sensitive. On decoder-only models such as GPT-3.5-Turbo and LLaMA-30B, the pa-
per compares models on MMLU, ARC-Challenge, and CommonsenseQA with zero-shot and five-shot
scenarios. Through "answer-moving attack" tests, the authors demonstrate significant performance vari-
ations depending on how correct answers are positioned spatially, and they determine that moving the
golden option to position D reduces GPT-3.5-Turbo accuracy significantly, but placing it at position A
enhances LLaMA-30B performance. To counteract this bias, the authors propose PriDe (Debiasing with
Prior Estimation), a label-free inference-time debiasing technique that decomposes prediction probabil-
ities into intrinsic content-based and prior position-based components using logarithmic decomposition
and permutation summation. With learning global priors on a limited number of samples, PriDe effec-
tively debiases future predictions at virtually no computational overhead and yields strong generalization
across similar domains. Although the strategy improves accuracy and stability with modest resources,
its benefit in performance degrades with huge permutation budgets. Further, PriDe generalization dete-
riorates with huge semantic gaps between domains, and its use extends only to some benchmarks and
low-shot regimes. Other prompting methods, like explicit debiasing and Chain-of-Thought reasoning,
fail to eliminate selection bias adequately.
Wang et al. (2023) carry out thorough evaluation of Unbalanced Matching (UM) bias and Distractor
Similarity (DS) bias in Visual-Language Understanding (VLU) long-form answer tasks (VQA-Long)
and find two most prevalent sources: UM bias, wherein correct answers are more n-gram similar to
inputs than distractors, and DS bias, wherein incorrect answers too close to each other but dissimilar to
the correct one. To counter these effects, the authors introduce Adversarial Data Synthesis (ADS) with
ADS-Text (ADS-T) and ADS-Image (ADS-I). ADS-T builds upon Adversarial Matching by adding vi-
13
sual regions from pre-trained CLIP embeddings and using ChatGPT-based distractor refinement guided
with human example supervision. ADS-I creates counterfactual image pairs via coarse-to-fine region
removal and triple-pass autoregressive inpainting on the SPL model. Training is complemented by Intra-
sample Counterfactual Training (ICT), with answer- and image-centric discriminative learning merged
for enhanced robustness. The ADS-ICT framework combined realizes consistent generalization across
a spectrum of visual-language models, realizing significant gains in debiased and domain-shifted condi-
tions while generating visually consistent, photorealistic counterfactuals. However, determining appro-
priate regions in ADS-I adds noise to affect synthesis quality and their reliance on ChatGPT as distractor
generation sources has inheritability drawbacks. Human validation steps raise research expenses, and
although the multi-component design is potent, it contributes to implementation difficulty. This work
investigates VCR, SNLI-VE, and VLEP datasets mostly, leaving generalizability to other VLU tasks
only partially mapped.

3.6
Response Analysis in Adaptive MCQ Systems
Corbett and Anderson (1995) offer a landmark investigation of response analysis through the develop-
ment of the ACT Programming Tutor, emphasizing modeling and tracing students’ knowledge states
during programming skill acquisition. Their knowledge tracing method continuously determines the
probability that a student has acquired certain rules in a cognitive model based on their interactive
responses while practicing. The system learns by offering individualized remedial drills to mastery,
including immediate feedback on correctness and guiding student advancement effectively. Empirical
tests validated the predictive power of knowledge tracing for performance both within the tutor and in fu-
ture assessments, emphasizing the importance of individual differences, parameter estimation, and error
analysis in adaptive modeling. This work enriched response analysis by linking sequential interactions
17
with probabilistic mastery estimates, enabling precise adaptive feedback in AI-based learning systems.
Piech et al. (2015) advanced this line of work by introducing Deep Knowledge Tracing (DKT), a
deep learning-based response analysis model utilizing Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Long
Short-Term Memory (LSTM) architectures. Unlike traditional knowledge tracing models that rely on
handcrafted domain representations and Markovian assumptions, DKT learns latent knowledge states
directly from interaction data. This enables accurate prediction of future student responses while un-
covering hidden relationships between exercises. Empirical evaluations using large-scale educational
datasets such as Khan Academy and Assistments demonstrated DKT’s significantly improved predic-
tion accuracy and its ability to support dynamic, personalized curriculum sequencing—thus marking a
paradigm shift in automated response analysis and adaptive learning.
Pandey and Karypis (2019) further refined this approach through the Self-Attentive Knowledge Trac-
ing (SAKT) model, which employs a Transformer-based self-attention mechanism to select and weigh
relevant portions of a learner’s historical interaction data for predicting subsequent performance. SAKT
improves interpretability and efficiency by dynamically focusing on useful components of past learning
traces instead of sequentially processing entire histories. Extensive experimentation across real-world
educational datasets revealed notable performance gains (average 4.43% increase in AUC) and better
scalability than RNN-based systems. Moreover, the attention maps produced by SAKT provide insight
into how specific past exercises influence future performance, facilitating fine-grained, interpretable re-
sponse analysis and targeted feedback delivery.
Choi et al. (2020) built upon these attention-based mechanisms with the introduction of SAINT,
a Transformer encoder-decoder model that separately processes exercise and response embeddings to
capture complex interactions between task features and learner behavior. SAINT integrates factors such
as exercise ID, category, correctness, response time, and timestamps to model temporal and contextual
relationships comprehensively. Tested on the large-scale EdNet dataset containing over 627,000 learners
and 72 million interactions, SAINT outperformed prior models (SAKT, NPA) in both accuracy and
AUC. Its multi-head attention visualizations reveal temporal dependencies that inform effective feedback
generation and adaptive curriculum design—positioning SAINT as a highly effective real-time response
analyzer for adaptive testing systems.
Shin et al. (2021) further enhanced this line of research with SAINT+, a Transformer-based architec-
ture incorporating temporal dynamics such as elapsed time (answer duration) and lag time (time between
learning activities) into response representations. By treating exercise and response information inde-
pendently within encoder-decoder layers and leveraging multi-head self-attention with upper triangular
masking, SAINT+ effectively captures the temporal order of interactions. Experimental results on the
EdNet-KT1 dataset—the largest publicly available education dataset—showed SAINT+ outperforms its
predecessors (SAINT and SAKT) with gains up to 2.72% in accuracy and 3.61% in AUC. Ablation stud-
ies validated the use of continuous embeddings for elapsed time and categorical embeddings for delay
time as optimal. The model’s ability to analyze response patterns and temporal behaviors enables highly
adaptive feedback creation and individualized learning path modeling.
Pu et al. (2024) proposed the Enhanced Latent-state Aggregation Knowledge Tracing (ELAKT)
model, an improved response analyzer that enhances latent knowledge state tracing by aggregating and
smoothing knowledge embeddings using encoder modules and causal convolutions. ELAKT captures
18
rich knowledge states at each timestep and manages stochastic student behavior such as slipping and
guessing through a multi-timestep prediction correction mechanism that forecasts response probabili-
ties over multiple future interactions. Experiments on large-scale educational datasets demonstrate that
ELAKT significantly surpasses state-of-the-art baselines such as DKT, SAKT, AKT, DKVMN, and
MAN across metrics like AUC, accuracy, MAE, and RMSE. Ablation studies confirm the effectiveness
of its input aggregation, hidden state smoothing, and multi-step prediction modules, highlighting its
robustness for real-time response analysis and adaptive feedback in intelligent tutoring systems.
Pardos et al. (2020) explored the integration of student response time in adaptive learning envi-
ronments, proposing models that incorporate temporal information alongside correctness to enhance
response analysis. Using large-scale educational datasets, their machine learning frameworks demon-
strated that including response times improves proficiency detection and adaptive feedback generation.
Their findings underscore the importance of considering multiple dimensions of learner interaction
data—such as correctness, response time, and behavioral patterns—to perform holistic response analysis
supporting personalized learning trajectories and real-time adaptive assessments.

Parcalabescu and Frank (2024) investigated the problem of assessing faithfulness in natural language

Jia et al. (2024) analyzed the fidelity of automated feedback provided by large language models on

Guo et al. (2024) created a multi-agent system named AutoFeedback to enhance the quality of AI-

remarks in line with students’ true performance. The research emphasizes the promise of multi-agent
teamwork for scalable and individualized learning aid and identifies challenges in completely addressing

Stamper et al. (2008) proposed the Hint Factory, a new usage of Markov decision processes for

of student solutions and assigns reward values to steer hint generation. The Hint Factory gives step-by-
step, goal-directed hints automatically and adjusts to the current state of the student’s problem. Their
pilot experiment in an undergraduate philosophy class showed high availability of hints and student use
of hints. This data-based hint generation process minimizes expert knowledge needed for intelligent
tutoring system development, allows for ongoing refinement with updated data, and facilitates adap-
tive feedback in non-adaptive computer-aided instruction settings. The research indicates promise for
machine learning applied to MDPs to create automated tutoring tools in other subject matters so that
personalized feedback is made more available and scalable for educational applications.
Steinert et al. (2024) presented LEAP, a system utilizing large language models such as GPT-4o
to provide research-grounded formative feedback designed to enhance students’ self-regulated learning.

Problem Statement
The conventional modes of assessment are usually based on inert question banks that do not address
different cognitive abilities of the learner and do not meet their changing mastery and learning require-
ments. Moreover, the current automated MCQ generation systems have problems including incompat-
ibility with pedagogical frameworks such as Bloom’s Taxonomy, deficiency of real-time flexibility in
response to student improvement, and susceptibility to biases in question material that can ruin objec-
tivity. The proposed research aims to overcome these drawbacks by creating an AI-based evaluation
platform that would be capable of dynamically creating unbiased, Bloom-compliant MCQs that react to
real-time student data, thereby enabling individualized, fair, and effective learning experiences.

The overall research question of the contribution of learning content would be how the systems of learn-
ing can could dynamically process and sieve and deliver quality teaching materials which would meet

multimedia teaching with a combination of the verbal channel with a visual channel results in better
outcomes and the teaching of the contents through unimodality has not been able to achieve the desired
22
results [42]. This can be explained by the fact that it is dual-channel thinking where learners simultane-

The technologies of machine learning and natural language processing have allowed developing
automated systems of content generation that can improve the performance and engagement of learners.
The available evidence suggests that AI-based learning resources can be used to positively influence
the test scores (about 23 points), the levels of engagement (about 31 points) compared to conventional
means of delivering the content [44]. These systems typically utilize knowledge representation models
to monitor the learner progress and also generate context sensitive micro-content. Despite the mentioned
advantages, AI-based generation processes are not free of limitations, including the factual inaccuracy,
inconsistency of the difficulty, and bias, which could be either cultural or linguistic. It is then suggested
to introduce hybrid approaches that would entail automated generation and evaluation of human experts
to guarantee quality of pedagogue, reliability and fairness.
Domain-specific studies once again describe the necessities of the pedagogical alignment. Digi-
tal resources well designed in the context of healthcare training have been demonstrated to be more
successful in terms of clinical assessment outcomes by 18 per cent in comparison with lectures [45].
Internal competent digital medical teaching in the majority of instances entails interactive simulation,
case-based reasoning, virtual patients, and intelligent tests according to the competency models. Other
areas of research are congruent in these findings, where digital content has been found most successful
in situations where it facilitates domain-specific reasoning behaviors and not to replace them. These
findings underline the fact that the role of education is mostly determined by the correspondence to the
goals of discipline and not technology in itself.
Team-based online learning is also useful in the appropriate application of content. Empirical stud-
ies prove that web-based applications such as Google Classroom, Microsoft Teams, and Padlet may be

[43]. Instructional structuring The role definition, formative interaction processes and balanced assess-

ability on one hand, pedagogical modeling on the opposite hand, quality assurance mechanisms and
career growth.

5.2
Content Preprocessing
The content preprocessing constitutes one of the pillars of the educational analytics. It is demonstrated
in experimental studies that structured pipelines can be preprocessed to improve the accuracy of the
analytics by 20-30 percent relative to the analysis of raw data [47]. Data cleaning (processing missing
values and data errors and duplicates), data transformation (normalization, encoding, feature construc-
tions), and feature selection and dimensionality reduction (also known as data reduction) is often part
of preprocessing. Special issues with educational dataset include hierarchical LMS, time interaction,
multi-source aggregation and high domain requirement. Preprocessing, in its turn, is an analytical, and
not a technical process.
Preprocessing rigor and predictive performance have been experimentally observed to be directly
related. Experience-based analysis of API indicates that neural networks, Naive Bayes classifiers and
decision trees can increase their accuracy by an average of 25-29, 22 and 67 percent respectively with
the assistance of structured preprocessing [48]. The behavioral predictors such as frequency of help-
seeking, access pattern of resources, discussing, and duration of engagement are always superior to the
demographic features, which imply the importance of interaction-based feature engineering.
According to the e-learning log case studies, log cleaning, temporal aggregation and learning-phase

5.3.1
Bloom’s Taxonomy Classifier
Bloom introduced three domains of learning objectives: cognitive, affective, and psychomotor. The cog-
nitive domain, which is central to assessment design, progresses from lower-order skills such as recall
and understanding to higher-order abilities including analysis, evaluation, and creation [4]. While the
psychomotor domain focuses on physical skills and the affective domain addresses attitudes and values
[1, 2], automated assessment systems predominantly operationalize the cognitive domain. However,
computational modeling of Bloom’s Taxonomy is inherently challenging due to the subjective bound-
aries between cognitive levels and linguistic ambiguity across disciplines [3, 5]. Action verbs frequently
overlap between levels, contextual intent varies by subject, and question phrasing may not explicitly
reflect cognitive complexity, making automated Bloom classification a complex natural language under-
standing problem.
Classical machine learning including Support Vector Machine (SVM), Naive Bayes, k-Nearest Neigh-
bor (k-NN), Logistic Regression, and Random Forest model predict the Bloom level by means of struc-
tured feature engineering, and supervised classification. The processing stage starts with text processing
such as tokenization, removing of stop-words, and stemming or lemmatization. TF-IDF vectors, n-
grams or frequency based matrices are used to convert the processed text into numerical representations.
Classifiers are fed on these feature vectors. SVM builds the best hyperplane in high dimensional space
to maximize the separation between classes, and the non-linear boundaries are usually addressed by the
application of kernel functions. Naive Bayes estimates posterior class likelihoods by applying the Bayes
law in conditional independence assumptions. k-NN labels by similarity (Euclidean or cosine distance)
by basing on the class that prevails in the sample neighborhood. The Logistic Regression models are
probabilities that are developed on weighted features and classified with the use of a sigmoid or softmax
function, which makes them probabilistic. Random Forest combines a set of decision trees which are
trained using bootstrapped samples and makes predictions by the majority voting to minimize variance.
These models are computationally cheap, interpretable based on feature weights or decision pathways
and work with smaller datasets. They are however very reliant on manual feature engineering, and they
have difficulties with contextual semantics and they tend to classify questions incorrectly when cognitive
verbs have overlapping lexical meanings but contextually contrast.
In order to compile strength, ensemble and hybrid models merge several base learners into a single
design. In bagging, various models are trained using various bootstrap samples and the predictions are
combined together to minimize variance. Training the models with trains in series, reweighting the
samples incorrectly labeled, with emphasis to the challenging cases, which minimizes bias. Stacking
adds a meta-learning layer in which predictions of heterogeneous base classifiers are used as input
features to a higher-level model which learns the best combination weights. This decision fusion is
more stable and predictive; this comes about by correcting individual model flaws. The problem with
ensemble frameworks however is that they raise computational complexity, limit transparency because
of interacting decision layers and it is complex to interpret in educational settings where explanatory
27
justification is valued.
Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and Bidirectional LSTM
(BiLSTM) Deep learning architectures eliminate manual feature extraction and learn representations.
Textual queries are represented as dense embeddings that encode semantic similarity by tokenizing and
converting them. CNN models are used, where local patterns (cognitive action phrases) are detected
using convolutional filters applied to sequence of embedding and the result max-pooled to retain salient
features and fully connected layers are used to classify. LSTM networks handle sequences with reg-
ulated information flow by using gated memory cells which can capture long-term dependencies and
determine cognitive intent over a whole question and not an individual keyword. BiLSTM builds on this
mechanism by having text processed forward and backward to produce more rich contextual represen-
tations and then classifying the text finally. These architectures enhance sequential semantic modeling
much better and complex or long questions are better addressed than the classical approaches. However,
they need sizeable labeled data, more computing power, and a sensitive choice of hyperparameters and
have little interpretability since they use distributed internal representations.
Transformer-based networks like BERT and RoBERTa use a self-attention mechanism that learns
to depict the relationship between all the tokens at once. Input text is first tokenized and positional
encodings are added and then multi-head self-attention calculates contextual interactions using query,
key, and value matrices. This allows the model to describe subtle intent of cognitions and long term
dependencies without regard to order constraints in sequence. In the fine-tuning stage, a layer that
classifies a set of data is added to the existing encoder and the supervised backpropagation is used
to modify the model parameters in order to do Bloom level prediction. Transformer models are very
accurate in Bloom classification because the pretrainfine-tune paradigm offers high levels of semantic
generalization and resistance to a wide range of linguistic backgrounds. Nevertheless, they contain
millions of parameters, are difficult to compute without a graphics card, are very memory-intensive, do
not have a high level of pedagogical interpretability, and can suffer performance loss when learning a
different domain across curricula or academic subjects.
Justifiable probabilistic methods like Labeled Latent Dirichlet Allocation (L-LDA) model Bloom
can be classified using supervised topic modeling. The questions are expressed as a combination of
latent topics with predetermined Bloom labels. During training, word distributions are linked to la-
beled topics, and inference makes an estimate of the likelihood of each of the cognitive levels through
topic proportions in the question. This probabilistic generative model offers explicit topic-word based
and understandable reasoning paths. Predictive accuracy is usually however less than discriminative
deep learning or transformer models and model assumptions can constrain its capacity to acquire deep
contextual semantics.
Comparing all types of machine learning models in detail, classical machine learning models, en-
semble models, deep learning models, transformer-based models, and probabilistic topic models are
radically different in terms of representation strategy, data dependency, computational requirements, in-
terpretability, scalability, and domain resistance. Classical models (SVM, Naivé Bayes, k-NN, Logistic
Regression, Random Forest) are trained on explicitly designed features; they are computationally effi-
cient and do not demand many hardware resources and the decision logic can be traced because of feature
weights or tree paths. Of them, SVM works well in high dimensional sparse spaces, but is sensitive with

respect to choice of its kernel; Naive bayes works fast and is stable with small datasets but is limited
by independence assumptions; k-nn is simple and non-parametric and yet computationally expensive
at inference time; Logistic Regression offers probabilistic interpretability but cannot follow nonlinear
boundaries; random forest can reduce variance and generalize better, but lacks transparency relative to
single-tree models. Bagging (also known as ensemble methods, or in other words: boosting, stacking)
is a predictive stability technique which combats predictive variance in a task by relying on multiple
classical predictors which are trained together on the same task, thereby increasing the tradeoff between
predictive stability and predictive bias in the resulting model, bagging versus boosting versus stacking:

5.3.2
Bias Detection
5.3.2.1
Bias Types
Positional Bias (also known as Option-Identifier Bias) [68] is the bias found in Large Language Models
over particular locations for the answer or identifiers (such as ‘A’ or ‘C’) irrespective of whether the
semantically correct answer is correct or not. Experiments have been able to show that with the same
semantically correct answer, varied performance levels with high values of difference being achieved
29
with position-shifting of the semantically correct answer across different locations for the answer. This
obviously states that the performance levels of the model are dependent on the location of the answer
rather than being dependent on the semantically correct answer.
The Anchoring Bias [67] is considered to be quite strong variation of Position Bias in which the
model strongly prefers the first option, ignoring the fact that the correct answer is provided in other
options because in this situation, the probability value of the first option is set to be extremely high,
ignoring the semantic indicator against it. The model is vulnerable to "positional tokens in the first few
options," leading to "the generation of the first hypothesis."
MCSB [66] is compromised in situations where this kind of binding between the abstract option
symbols, for instance, A/B/C/., and their respective contents is not complete or is not stable. For instance,
if there is poor binding between the symbols, then there will be ability to recognize what to put in the
answer while, at the same time, spitting out another label for another option based on preferences at the
symbol level. Consequently, this will result in predictions which will be highly sensitive to apparent
variations, for instance, naming and ordering options, while there are actually no variations in semantic
contents.
Unbalanced Matching bias takes place when correct responses have a higher degree of overlap in
form of n-gram with the questions or with the images their answers are with compared to those of in-
correct options, such as the latter (Bampers, 2010, p. 955) [66]. Furthermore, distractors usually have a
higher number of irrelevant n-grams than correct responses with non-relevant n grams to correct gram-
mars average 1.14 and correct grammars average 0.18. This trend demonstrates that lexical superficiality
can inappropriately exaggerate the apparent model knowledge.
Distractor Similarity bias occurs when distractor choices are too similar between themselves and
too different between the correct choice and the distractor choice that allows models to recognize the
correct choice even in the absence of context is called Distractor Similarity bias [69]. Quantitative
similarity tests reveal that the average intra-sample similarity between distractors and correct answers
is 0.31, and average inter-sample similarity between correct answer and other correct answers are 0.34,
which suggest that correct answer tends to be similar to other correct answers more than they are to
their distractors. The mean intra-sample homogeneity of distractors is 0.36 which indicates unnatural
homogeneity that decreases model discriminative ability.
5.3.2.2
Detection Metrics
The standard deviation of recalls (RStd) can be used as a numerical expression to take a quantitative
measure of selection bias based on the balance of alternative positions [68]. It is a natural metric, with
the greater the imbalance in recalls, the greater the selection bias and the measure is not influenced by
label imbalance as simple counting measures. recall balance is also showcased as a marker of the model
resilience to changes in option positions and it has been proven through a strong correlation and balance
between resiliency to answer-moving aggression and balance across position recalls in initial inputs.
Answer-Moving Diagnostics [66] involves the systematic rearrangement of the correct answer among
the options without any change in the content of the question and the correct answer. Variation in ac-
curacy within these settings provides a clear measure of the dependency between the predicted output
and the order of the options. A large variation in accuracy measures the bias of selection or anchoring.
30
This is an appropriate diagnostic test since it measures positional bias without modifying the level of
difficulty in the test.
Permutation Consistency Measures [68] are used for testing how well interaction between symbols
and contents is bound on inspecting how well the same answer content is always picked without consid-
ering the permutation of options. This actually tests the proportion of times for which the permutation is
unchanged, which will presumably have high stability for high values and a lack of robustness for low
values in terms of the binding of option symbols to their contents.
5.3.2.3
Mitigation Techniques
PriDe reduces selection bias by breaking down the observed prediction distribution into a two part-
intrinsic prediction distribution and a prior preference distribution over option identifiers [68]. These
estimated global priors are later reused to debias future samples. In low-cost situations, PriDe offers
better debiasing efficiency than Full or Cyclic Permutation baselines, and matches or even better perfor-
mance. It can be trained label-free with insignificant computational cost in an inference step following
initial estimation, and exhibits high cross-domain generalization where priors trained in a single MMLU
domain transfer well to different domains. Moreover, the estimated priors do not change with the change
in sample size, and similar domains have the same accuracy improvements.
SCB improves the Multiple Choice Symbol Binding (MCSB) capacity, as both the option symbols
and contents are used to train the subjects of the experiment. SCB is the one that incorporates both
the option symbols and the contents as training targets [66]. SCB does not however always minimize
selection bias and in some cases, it inflates it in the case of the LLaMA-2-13B model. RSCB builds
upon SCB with reweighting on Focal loss with the principle of label words being anchors. RSCB per-
forms better to the extent of improving MCSB capacity and thereby minimizing selection bias since the
importance of symbols is prioritized in training. The construction of negative samples in PIF is based
on the random pairing of the incorrect option content with all other symbol options and a point-wise
loss function based on Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) [66]. The optimization
objective is to have the probability of positive examples near to 1 as much as possible and the probability
of negatives nearer to less than the value of ε as little as possible. Factually, PIF minimizes selection
bias (µbias). The approach is generalizable to all types of LLM architectures without having to alter the
structure. Nonetheless, PIF necessitates warm-up initiation with RSCB parameters and also increases
the level of implementation complexity since a single example negative is randomly selected per training
instance. In this method, internal representations are directly modified by changing values vectors also
referred to as, v,n in MLP layers, directly [67]. Such updates yield significant improvement in classifica-
tion accuracy in MCQ data collections. The approach involves limited intervention, changes in selective
attention heads and value vectors, without retraining or changing architecture. Non-MCQ task perfor-
mance however is impaired due to direct value vectors manipulation, as demonstrated by deterioration
on original IOI benchmarks and Greater-than benchmarks.
ADS produces artificial factual and counterfactual image-text information to alleviate Unbal anced
Matching (UM) and Distractor Similarity (DS) biases [69]. ADS-Text (ADS-T) reinforces Adversar-
ial Matching by visual premise evidence and visual relevance scoring by pretrained CLIP models, and
chatbot-based refinement of distractors through human-outlined samples. There is a high level of gen-
31
eralization in visual-language (VL) models in ADS-ICT. More advantages are seen with domain-shifted
conditions.

A dynamic question engine is used to select and order assessment items in order to accommodate learner
characteristics [53]. Instead of giving a fixed test, adaptive systems alter the difficulty of items in order

Research in computerized adaptive testing indicates that the test length can be cut by 40-60 percent
without affecting test measurement. The selection methods used often maximize the Fisher information
with regard to estimated ability. More modern implementations use machine learning in estimating that
the abilities, and multi-objective optimization to find a balance between coverage, fairness, and exposure
management.
The deep knowledge tracing models, such as DKT, SAKT, and KQN have been shown to have better
predictive power than conventional probabilistic approaches [54]. Massive datasets demonstrate useful

Recurrent neural network models have demonstrated to achieve up to 25 times greater predictive
accuracy when compared to the previous probabilistic models [55]. Although they are very precise, they
consume a large amount of data and calculations.
The models of knowledge tracing that are enhanced with the learning-curve feature demonstrate

The systematic reviews of AI tutoring on more than 30,000 learners report the effect sizes ranging

The challenges associated with implementation are scalability, calibration requirements and institutional

Conclusion
This survey provides a detailed discussion of adaptive multiple-choice assessment systems mediated by
artificial intelligence and organized around the Bloom Taxonomy and real-time performance analytics
of a learner. The review illustrates how artificial intelligence, in the form of machine learning, deep
learning, transformer architecture, and large language models, has restructured conventional evaluation
methods of the past as dynamic, customized, and continually changing evaluation ecosystems. Contem-
porary adaptive systems, in contrast to traditional fixed test banks, combine automated MCQ creation,
cognitive-level classification, bias detection, adaptive sequencing, and smart feedback, into a closed-
loop design. The gathered evidence throughout the literature attests to the fact that AI-based assessment
systems offer tremendous benefits in terms of the level of personalization, accuracy of classification, and
efficiency and lessen the time of assessment without impacting measurement accuracy.
This article adds a synthesized and comparative knowledge of the available methods as opposed
to considering each layer separately. The evidence examined shows that there is no single modeling
paradigm that is eminent in all aspects in adaptive assessment systems. Classical machine learning
models offer predictable and understandable Blooms level classification accuracy (85-90) with success-
ful feature engineering, in comparison with better contextual understanding of semantically challeng-
ing questions, albeit at greater computational expense, offered by transformer-based and hybrid deep
33
learning models. Proprietary large language models are more linguistically fluent in MCQ generation,
whereas open-weight and curriculum-aligned retrieval-augmented models are also more transparent and
curriculum-aligned. Symbol-Content Binding and PriDe are bias mitigation methods capable of alle-
viating measurably biasing instances of bias, but bias mitigation performance is architecture-specific.
Through comparative study of the classification, generation, control of bias, knowledge tracing as well
as feedback process through a single integrated framework, this survey provides a consolidated base
to researchers, which notes the architectural trade-offs, system-level strategies of integration and open
solution problems in adaptive assessment research.
The focus in future studies should be on interpretability, cross-domain generalizability, fairness, and
control of hallucinations to make sure that they can be successfully deployed in any high-stakes edu-
cational setting. Adaptive sequencing and mastery modeling can be enhanced additional by increased
integration of cognitive, behavioral, and affective signals of the learner. Also, the explainable AI pro-
cesses and effective bias diagnostics are supposed to be integrated at the generation and evaluation levels
to provide transparency and pedagogical integrity. The development of AI-based adaptive MCQ systems
is an indication of a paradigm shift to smart and learner-centered and scalable assessment ecosystems
and future interdisciplinary cooperation will be paramount to making AI-based assessment systems ful-
fill their full educational capabilities.
Future Scope
The increasing interdependence of artificial intelligence, cognitive science, and educational technology
is driving significant advancements in adaptive MCQ-based assessment systems. While current systems
can scale using advanced machine learning models and large language models (LLMs) to provide auto-
mated, personalized feedback, several promising research directions can further enhance their capability,
fairness, and scalability.
One important direction is the development of explainable cognitive-level classification models that
clearly justify question difficulty and Bloom-level allocation. Such interpretability will strengthen edu-
cator trust and help students better understand gaps in their cognitive learning progression.
Future systems may also integrate multimodal learning signals such as handwriting patterns, speech
input, eye-tracking data, and emotional state recognition. Extending adaptation beyond knowledge ac-
curacy toward holistic learner profiling will enable systems to respond not only to correctness but also to
engagement, frustration, fatigue, or disengagement. Affective feedback mechanisms could dynamically
adjust question pacing and delivery to improve learning efficiency.
Advanced architectures combining LLMs with knowledge tracing models and reinforcement learn-
ing can move beyond static recommendation systems toward intelligent tutoring agents that continuously
optimize question strategies based on long-term learner development. Multi-agent LLM frameworks for
automated question validation, cognitive tagging, plagiarism detection, and distractor quality scoring
can reduce human workload while improving robustness and reliability.
Bias detection and mitigation remain critical research areas. On-the-fly fairness auditing, domain-
aware debiasing techniques, cultural sensitivity modeling, and internationally diverse training datasets
can strengthen equity across languages, disciplines, and demographic groups. Standardization of evalu-
34
ation benchmarks for educational LLMs, Bloom classifiers, and adaptive engines will also be necessary
to ensure interpretability and cross-institutional reliability.
From a deployment perspective, privacy-preserving adaptive assessment systems incorporating edge
computing can secure sensitive learner data while maintaining scalability in large academic environ-
ments. Integration with Learning Management Systems (LMS), classroom dashboards, and institutional
analytics platforms will enable real-time insights and actionable intervention strategies for educators.
Ultimately, the future of AI-driven adaptive MCQ systems lies in building open, intelligent, emotion-
ally aware, and bias-resistant learning ecosystems that evolve continuously with learners and empower
educators to deliver high-quality, personalized education at a global scale.
